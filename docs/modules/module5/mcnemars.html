McNemar’s Test

McNemar’s test is the categorical analog of the paired t-test: it’s used when a binary outcome is measured twice on the same subjects (e.g., before and after an intervention), rather than on two independent groups.

The key insight is that concordant pairs (subjects who gave the same answer both times) don’t tell us anything about change—only the discordant pairs (subjects whose status switched) matter. McNemar’s test asks: among the discordant pairs, is one direction of switching more common than the other?

\[H_0: \text{switching in each direction is equally likely} \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_A: \text{one direction is more common}\]

Motivating example: A clinic measured flu antibody protection status (Protected / Not Protected) in 120 patients, both before and after a booster shot. We want to know: did the booster shift patients toward being protected?

The results were:

After: Protected After: Not Protected
Before: Protected 40 10
Before: Not Protected 35 35

Setting Up the Table

# table of before/after protection status
tbl = matrix(c(40, 10, 35, 35), nrow = 2, byrow = T)
rownames(tbl) = c("Before: Protected", "Before: Not Protected")
colnames(tbl) = c("After: Protected", "After: Not Protected")
tbl

Notice the two diagonal cells (40 and 35) are the concordant pairs (status didn’t change), while the two off-diagonal cells (10 and 35) are the discordant pairs (status changed in one direction or the other).

Out of the 120 patients, how many had their protection status change between before and after the booster? Why do the 40 patients who were “Protected” both times, and the 35 who were “Not Protected” both times, not help us determine whether the booster had an effect?

Calculating the Test Statistic by Hand

McNemar’s test statistic (without a continuity correction) is:

\[\chi^2 = \dfrac{(b - c)^2}{b + c}\]

where \(b\) and \(c\) are the two discordant cell counts (here, \(b = 10\) patients went from Protected to Not Protected, and \(c = 35\) went from Not Protected to Protected).

A very small p-value—strong evidence that switching happened more often in one direction (Not Protected → Protected) than the other, consistent with the booster having a real effect.

An Equivalent View: Testing a Proportion

McNemar’s test is actually equivalent to testing whether the proportion of discordant pairs going in one particular direction equals 0.5—i.e., a one-proportion test applied only to the discordant pairs!

Fill in the code below to run this as a one-proportion z-test: out of the 45 discordant pairs (\(b + c = 10 + 35 = 45\)), test whether the proportion that went from Not Protected to Protected (\(c = 35\)) differs from 0.5.

Solution 
# one-proportion test on the discordant pairs
prop.test(x = 35, n = 45, p = 0.5, correct = F)

The test statistic here, when squared (\(z^2\)), should come out to the same value as our McNemar chi-squared statistic (13.889)—another example of the \(z^2 = \chi^2\) relationship we saw with the two-proportion z-test and chi-squared test of homogeneity.

McNemar’s test, a one-proportion test on the discordant pairs, and even a paired t-test (if we coded “Protected” as 1 and “Not Protected” as 0) are all closely related ways of analyzing paired binary data. Why do we specifically exclude the concordant pairs when testing for a change in protection status?
